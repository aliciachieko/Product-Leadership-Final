# Individual Insights: Strategy Development Process

> Module 6 · Leverage AI Tools for Leadership Execution

## Friction points

The hardest part of building this strategy was not the strategy itself; it was turning "we collaborate" into decision rules that survive a real disagreement.

The mission one-liner was the last blank in the whole team charter. It was easy to draft a sentence everyone nods at; it was much harder to write one sentence that still holds up against KR1, KR2, and KR3. The same friction showed up in the decision table: ownership areas looked tidy on paper, but the moment I had to say who actually *decides* when two PMs disagree, the tidy version fell apart.

How I worked through it: I forced everything into one row per decision, named a default rule (PMs decide collaboratively or defer to the correct PM, and escalate to the Core Product Lead when there is no agreement), and put clocks on the escalations, a decision within 3 business days, and within 1 week for conflicts that affect quarterly commitments. Writing the tie-break rule in advance, instead of relying on whoever argues best in the room, is what made the charter usable.

The same lesson repeated in the strategy document itself. My first KR3 measured users opting into a feature — an output dressed up as an outcome and it took the AI pressure test to catch it. Rewriting it as successful re-engagement (30% to 55%) and moving the opt-in measure to a learning objective made the whole OKR cascade more honest.

## Key learnings

- **Write the kill criteria when you write the business case, not after.** Mine has dates — KR1 and KR2 checkpoints on June 30, 2027, KR3 on September 30, 2027 and a guardrail that overrides the numbers entirely: complaints or opt-outs above 2% in any month pause the AI prompts immediately. Deciding the exit in advance is what makes a bet safe to fund.
- **Outcomes over outputs, everywhere.** Every roadmap bet carries an owning team and a success signal, not just a feature. If a Now item can't be traced back to a KR, it doesn't belong in the quarter.
- **The hard no does as much work as the bet.** Saying out loud where we will not play: no engagement mechanics, no content library, no B2B play this year; focused the strategy more than any addition did.
- **Flag modeled numbers as modeled.** CAC of $54 and LTV rising from $118 to $171 are assumptions to validate, not facts. Naming that plainly made the business case more credible, not less — a skeptical reader trusts a case that knows its own weak points.
- **Use AI as the devil's advocate before the room does.** The pressure-test prompt found my weakest assumption (that users who feel better still want an ongoing relationship with Fable) and my output-in-disguise KR faster than my own review did. I now build the attack into the process: pressure-test the strategy, and review work from the point of view of the people I have to convince, the Head of Product, asking whether it advances company strategy, the Growth Lead asking what it does to acquisition — before I am in the room with them.
- **A charter is an operating tool, not documentation.** I used to treat it as paperwork that follows the real decisions. This project reversed that: the charter's decision rules are the thing that makes every later decision faster.

## "Aha!" moment

My aha was realizing that churn is a success problem, not a failure problem.

Users don't leave Fable because the product failed; they leave because it worked. The acute need ends, and so does the relationship. That single realization reorganized everything: the problem stopped being onboarding or engagement and became the moment *after* success. It is why the strategy is built around retaining users after their acute need is resolved, and why the biggest bet of personalizing support using wellness memory is about confidence, proactive action, and successful re-engagement rather than more features or more time in the app.
